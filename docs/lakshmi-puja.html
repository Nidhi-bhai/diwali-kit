Lakshmi Puja, in the order a household can actually do it

This is a short home puja for Sunday 8 November 2026, written for people doing Diwali outside India. It is not the only vidhi, and it is not a priest's manual. If your family has a sequence, use that.

On Drik Panchang, Lakshmi Puja falls on Sunday 8 November 2026 in every city in the table below. The clock time is local, and it is not the same city to city. The labeled "Lakshmi Puja Muhurat" on those pages is the part of Vrishabha (a fixed lagna) that sits inside Pradosh Kaal, the evening window after sunset. Where Vrishabha runs past the end of Pradosh, Drik's muhurat ends when Pradosh ends. That happens on the Sydney, Melbourne, Auckland, and Singapore pages.

Before you start

Pick the row for your city. Then ask your temple if that is the window they are using. A rented flat may not allow incense, camphor, or an open flame. A lamp you can switch off is enough if a flame is not allowed. Tell the people you live with, and put out every flame before you sleep or leave.

Steps

  1. Clean a small corner. Lay a cloth. Place a picture of Lakshmi, and of Ganesha if you have one. East is the usual direction. Another quiet wall is fine if the room does not face that way.
  2. Fill a clean jug with water and drop in a coin. Set the jug beside the pictures. This stands in for a kalash.
  3. On a plate, keep raw rice, a little turmeric or kumkum, one or two flowers, a fruit, and a sweet.
  4. Light a ghee or oil lamp. If you cannot have a flame, switch on a small lamp and continue.
  5. Sit down. In your own words, say your name, the date, and that you are doing Lakshmi Puja. That spoken intention is the sankalp.
  6. Offer a spoon of water onto a small dish, then a few grains of rice, a flower, and the sweet. Do this facing the pictures.
  7. If you know a mantra, recite it. If you do not, sit quietly or say a short prayer in English. The printable kit has Devanagari, transliteration, and meanings for a household set.
  8. If it is safe, place a lamp near the door or a window. If it is not, one lamp at the pictures is the whole lighting.
  9. Move a plate holding the lamp in a slow circle in front of the pictures. That is the aarti. Then bow.
  10. Share the sweet. Tidy the corner. Extinguish flames you cannot watch.

What you need, and what to buy abroad

These substitutes are household practice. They are not a ruling that every tradition accepts them.

  • Lamp. A small metal bowl, cotton wool rolled into a wick, and ghee or any food oil. A tea light works. If the building forbids flames, use a lamp with a switch.
  • Ghee. Supermarkets and Indian grocers sell it. Oil is the backup for the lamp. Butter is a poor lamp fuel.
  • Kumkum. Indian shops sell it. A pinch of turmeric is the usual stand-in. Skip glitter.
  • Rice. Any raw rice, for the offering called akshat.
  • Flowers. Marigold if you can find it. Any fresh flower otherwise. Skip plastic flowers.
  • Kalash. A copper pot if you own one. Otherwise a clean jug or a drinking glass, with a coin in the water. Mango leaves are traditional on the mouth of the pot. Any clean leaves, or none, will do.
  • Camphor and incense. Skip both if you do not have them or if smoke is a problem. The lamp is the aarti.
  • Sweet and fruit. Mithai from an Indian grocer, or any vegetarian sweet you offer with attention, plus a fruit you would eat.
  • Cloth. A clean scarf or napkin. Red and yellow are customary. A plain clean cloth is fine.
  • Image. A printed picture is the normal home substitute for a murti.
  • Rangoli. Optional. Flour on a plate, or chalk on paper, is enough if you want a mark at the door.
  • Account book. Some families place a notebook or the year's first bills beside Lakshmi. A notebook is enough.
